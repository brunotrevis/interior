Cover story · Cities · Guarapuava, Brazil

The city that keeps its frost

More than a thousand metres up on the Paraná plateau, Guarapuava has made peace with the cold, and built its public life around a lake, a wood fire and a shared gourd of mate.

Words: Interior DeskPhotography: Prefeitura de Guarapuava9 min read
The city in brief
State
Paraná, southern Brazil
Population
Some 180,000
Altitude
Over 1,000 metres
From Curitiba
Around 250 km west by road
Go for
Frost mornings, June to August

At seven in the morning in July, the grass on the edge of town is not green. It is white, stiff, and it crunches underfoot. Frost settles on fence posts and fallen leaves and the roofs of parked cars, and for an hour the valley looks as if someone has dusted it with sugar. Then the sun clears the ridge, the white turns to steam, and Guarapuava goes to work.

Most people outside Brazil, and plenty inside it, could not place this city on a map. It sits in the centre-south of Paraná, on a high plateau where the land rolls in long green waves between stands of araucaria pine. Some 180,000 people live here. It is not a capital, not a port and not a resort. It is the kind of place this magazine was made for: a city of the middle, large enough to have opinions and small enough to hear them.

Altitude as a way of life

Altitude explains a lot. At more than a thousand metres, the winters are sharp by Brazilian standards, and the city has built its habits around them. Houses have wood stoves. Scarves come out in May. Winter festivals fill the calendar just as coastal cities empty their beaches. People talk about the weather not as small talk but as news.

The cold shapes the landscape too. The araucaria, a tall conifer with a flat, candelabra crown that once covered much of southern Brazil, still punctuates the horizon here, standing above the lower trees like a second skyline. It is the tree on the postcards and the tree you notice first from the road: a reminder that Brazil also has highlands, and that they look like nowhere else.

A lake in the middle of everything

Seen from above, the centre is organised around water. A lake sits inside a rectangle of avenues, ringed by a footpath, lawns and old trees. On a weekday morning it belongs to walkers and runners; by late afternoon, to families on the grass and teenagers on the low walls. On a Sunday with an event on, it belongs to everyone at once.

Urbanists call places like this third places: neither home nor work, somewhere to meet without having to buy anything. Many cities have to design them from scratch. Here the habit seems to have grown on its own, out of sitting outside whenever the sun allows, which in a cold city is an occasion rather than a given.

The shared gourd

If the lake is the city's living room, chimarrão is its handshake. The bitter green mate, drunk hot through a metal straw from a hollowed gourd, passes from hand to hand in parks, offices and doorways. The etiquette is precise: whoever prepares it drinks first, the gourd travels round the circle, and you say thank you only when you have had enough.

It is easy to file a ritual like this under folklore. It is better understood as infrastructure. A gourd that has to be refilled keeps people in one place for half an hour. It turns strangers into a circle. On a winter afternoon, it is also the warmest thing in the park.

The same gaúcho culture that gives the region its mate fills its dance halls and festivals: wide red skirts, leather boots, an accordion somewhere nearby. Young dancers rehearse for months. The performances are proud and a little theatrical, and they draw a crowd that spans three generations.

Old walls, new glass

Mid-sized cities often face a blunt choice about their older buildings: knock them down for something taller, or let them decay politely. Guarapuava has examples of a third way. In the centre, a two-storey brick hall with arched windows has been restored and joined to a tall glass volume that repeats its gabled roofline. On the edge of town, a greenhouse of curved glass and white steel looks, from the right angle, like a ship about to leave. Neither is famous. Both belong to a city that wants to be looked at.

Blossom in the cold

Then comes the surprise of late winter. Along the lake and in private gardens, ornamental cherry trees flower while the nights are still cold, and for a few weeks the city is pink against a hard blue sky. Hummingbirds work the branches. Photographers turn up before breakfast. It is a short season, which is exactly why people go out to see it.

None of this makes Guarapuava a destination in the way brochures use the word, and that is not the point. The point is that a city of this size, far from the coast and the capitals, can offer a complete life: a centre you can walk, a landscape with character, rituals that bring strangers together, and weather that gives the year a shape. That is what we will be looking for, city by city, in the issues to come.
